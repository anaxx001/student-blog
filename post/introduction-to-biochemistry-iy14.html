Introduction to Biochemistry

Anaxx·6 Nov 2026·6 min read
Not to make it sound too touchy or abstruse, but before delving into topics like cells, glycolysis, nucleic acids and so on, I think it's best to first understand the concept, aim and purpose of biochemistry as a course and discipline.


 I strongly believe that the first step towards understanding any field or course is by first understanding its roots. By this, I mean having a proper understanding of the basic concepts behind the course, especially when dealing with a course like biochemistry.



I believe this helps you understand what exactly you're studying and gives you a better perspective of the subject. There are several courses like anatomy, histology, embryology, gross anatomy, etc., and each comes with its own concepts and areas of focus. Going through all the unnecessary information without first understanding the basics can make things unnecessarily complicated.



So, before we begin, there are a few questions I'd like us to consider:


  •  What exactly is biochemistry?

  •  How is it different from physiology? (If you haven't noticed the similarities between them, never mind.)

  •  Of what importance is biochemistry to basic medical sciences?


These questions and a few others I'll do my very best to answer.


This might end up being a long read, so I'd advise you to find somewhere calm and settled before you start reading this.


Now, back to business.

What exactly is Biochemistry?


Hmm, where do I begin?


Biochemistry is simply the chemistry of life. That's the short but precise way of putting it. I mean, that's what I learnt from ChatGPT and a few YouTube videos. Lol.


Remember when I said it's the chemistry of life? It includes all and every living thing. However, this blog's focus is only on us and our species. (I'm mostly referring to humans, though my apologies if you're an alien species with sentience.)


So, have you thought about what happens when you eat food and eventually get satisfied?


Or why your muscles feel sore when you work out?


Or how and why your wound suddenly stops bleeding after some time, eventually forming a clot and a scab?


Well, of course you have (thought about or questioned some of these things before).


Now, for most of us familiar with physiology and the body's various functions, we can explain quite a lot about the processes happening within us. But if physiology alone can explain what happens, what about the chemical processes responsible for these activities?


Most of these are physiological responses of your body. (I'll touch on that in a different article.)


Biochemistry explains the chemical processes behind the biological activities that occur in our bodies.


In a way, biochemistry is the middle ground between biology and chemistry.


Based on what I've come to understand, I think biochemistry is a branch of science that seeks to explain the chemical reactions and processes behind the biological activities that occur within the body, such as digestion, metabolism and so on.


But if you want a textbook definition, then:


Biochemistry is the branch of science that studies the chemical substances, reactions and processes that occur in living organisms.


So, at this point, I believe we've been able to establish what biochemistry is.




What makes Biochemistry different from Physiology?



Well, the answer to this is very simple, but before that, let's define physiology.


Physiology is the branch of biological science that studies how living organisms and their various parts function.


Physiology focuses on how different tissues, organs and systems in the body work to maintain life.


It explains how each organ functions, what a cell or group of cells is responsible for, and how different systems interact to keep the body functioning properly.


Biochemistry, on the other hand, focuses on the chemical processes behind the functioning of these organs and systems.


It explains the chemical reactions that take place in the production of bicarbonate in the pancreas, the pathways responsible for generating ATP, how food is broken down and how much energy is released in the process, among other things.


It is important to understand that while biochemistry accounts for the chemical reactions that occur behind biological activities, it doesn't necessarily account for every physiological activity itself.


The two subjects are closely related, and their areas of study sometimes overlap. However, their major areas of emphasis are different.




Importance of Biochemistry to Basic Medical Sciences



And finally, the importance of biochemistry to basic medical sciences.


I'm a bit tired from writing all of this, so I'll only be listing a few points here. We can always explore them in greater detail some other time.


1. Understanding normal physiology:​​

Biochemistry helps us understand the chemical processes responsible for normal physiological functions. It gives us a better understanding of how different cells, tissues and organs carry out their activities.


2. ​​Understanding disease:

Many diseases are associated with abnormalities in biochemical processes. Studying biochemistry helps us understand the chemical and molecular changes that occur in the body during disease conditions.


3. ​Understanding Metabolism: 

Biochemistry helps us understand how food is broken down, how nutrients are utilised and how energy is produced and stored in the body.


4. ​Clinical Diagnosis:

Certain biochemical substances, enzymes and other components can be measured in blood and other body fluids to help diagnose and monitor diseases.


5. Understanding the action of drugs:

Biochemistry helps us understand how drugs interact with different molecules in the body and how these interactions produce their effects.


These are just a few examples. There are several other ways biochemistry contributes to medical science.

Biomolecules


Now, before we conclude, let's briefly introduce another important concept in biochemistry: 'biomolecules'.


What exactly are biomolecules?


Biomolecules are important molecules produced or found in living organisms that enable them to carry out the basic activities necessary to sustain life.


A more polished way of putting it would be:


Biomolecules are chemical substances found in living organisms that are essential for their structure, growth, maintenance and proper functioning.


There are four major classes of biomolecules:


1. Carbohydrates
2. Proteins
3. Lipids
4. Nucleic acids


And in biochemistry, these four classes of molecules are mostly going to be our primary focus.


Do note that there are also other biomolecules, but these four are the major ones.
